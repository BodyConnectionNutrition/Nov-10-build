A Body Connection guided learning experience

Who taught you to eat?

Trace the lessons that shaped how you understand hunger, food, health, appetite and bodies—then take one belief apart closely enough to see where it came from.

This is not a test of whether your family taught you correctly. We learn to eat through relationships, culture, institutions, economics and experience. The goal is to make that learning visible enough to examine.

A belief can feel self-evident long after we have forgotten where we learned it.

Part 4 · Lessons are not all the same kind of thing

Separate a rule from a fact.

Food beliefs often blend biological observations, cultural norms, family adaptations, moral judgments, personal preferences, and scientific claims. Before deciding whether a belief is useful, identify what kind of claim it actually is.

Choose one belief you want to examine.

Part 5 · Context, not villains

What problem might this lesson have been trying to solve?

Origin does not determine truth.

A rule can have been understandable, caring, or adaptive in one context and still deserve re-examination in another. The goal is not to identify who was wrong. It is to recover the context that disappeared when the rule began to feel universal.

What taught you to eat?

The educational reveal

The ideas that feel most personal may have long histories. Some began as family adaptations. Some came from culture, medicine, dieting, marketing, economics, or repeated experience. Some were never spoken aloud.

Learning where a framework came from does not automatically tell you whether it is true. It gives you the next question: does this belief still fit the evidence, your body, your values, and the life you are actually living?

This is education, not therapy or diagnosis. It supports critical reflection on learned food frameworks; it does not assign blame, diagnose a condition, or determine what you should eat.
