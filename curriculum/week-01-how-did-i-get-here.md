# Week 1 — How Did I Get Here?

## Core idea

**A relationship has a history.**

## Guiding question

**How did I learn to think about food and my body this way?**

## Participant shift

**“This is simply how I think about food and my body.”**

→

**“The way I think about food and my body has been shaped over time.”**

Week 1 does not ask participants to decide which influences were good or bad, challenge beliefs, change eating behavior, improve body image, or replace old ideas with new ones. Its job is more fundamental: help participants recognize that their present food-and-body framework has a history.

## Story architecture

**Story → Concept → Experience → Reflection → Meaning**

Nora’s grocery-store story is the narrative spine. Her question is introduced before the conceptual teaching, remains unresolved while participants examine their own histories, and returns at the end with greater context but without a prescribed answer.

---

# ACT I — THE GROCERY AISLE

## Nora: two cereal boxes

Nora is standing in the cereal aisle holding two boxes.

One is the cereal she actually wants.

The other is the cereal she thinks she **should** buy.

Nothing dramatic is happening. She is buying breakfast.

She turns one box over and reads the nutrition panel.

Calories.

Sugar.

Protein.

Fiber.

Ingredients.

Then she turns over the other box and compares.

One begins to feel responsible.

The other begins to feel indulgent.

She has not consciously decided to turn breakfast into a test of character. But somewhere in the comparison, the original question—**Which cereal do I want?**—has changed.

Now the question sounds more like:

**Which choice says the right thing about me?**

Imagine a small red card appearing between the two boxes.

## BE GOOD

And underneath it:

## WHO SAYS?

Do not answer that question yet.

For now, simply notice that a voice has entered an ordinary food decision.

### Pause

Think about the phrase **be good** around food.

What does “good” mean there?

Healthy? Disciplined? Restrained? Responsible? Thin? In control? Following the plan?

You do not need to decide whether any of those ideas are correct. Week 1 begins somewhere earlier:

**How did those meanings get there?**

# ACT II — THE FURNISHED ROOM

Imagine walking into a furnished room.

There is a sofa, a table, a rug, pictures on the wall, books on the shelves. If you live in the room long enough, eventually you stop noticing most of the furnishings.

They simply become **the room**.

Food and body frameworks can work the same way.

Over time, ideas enter:

- Finish what is on your plate.
- Dessert is a treat.
- Carbs are bad.
- Thin means healthy.
- You should not eat if you are not hungry.
- Weight gain means something has gone wrong.
- Exercise makes up for eating.
- Healthy people are disciplined.
- Certain bodies are more acceptable than others.
- My body should look different than it does.

Eventually, we may stop experiencing these as ideas that entered the room. They can begin to feel like the room itself.

## The framework was furnished over time

You did not enter adulthood with an empty food-and-body framework.

Many things may have participated in furnishing it:

- family
- caregivers
- peers
- school
- healthcare
- athletics
- religion
- culture
- media
- social media
- dieting
- food availability
- scarcity
- comments about bodies
- clothing
- puberty
- relationships
- illness
- pregnancy
- aging
- weight changes
- pleasure
- traditions
- your own experiences and conclusions

Some influences may have been explicit. Others may never have been spoken aloud.

Some may still carry enormous weight. Others may now feel distant.

Some may have been helpful. Some may have been painful. Many may be complicated.

Week 1 is not asking you to throw out the furniture.

**It is asking you to turn on the lights and look around.**

# ACT III — HOW FOOD ACQUIRED MEANING

Food has biological importance. Humans need energy and nutrients, and bodies participate continuously in eating.

But humans do not encounter food only as biology.

We encounter food inside families, communities, economies, traditions, relationships, institutions, and cultures.

Food can acquire meanings such as:

- care
- celebration
- reward
- discipline
- belonging
- identity
- morality
- scarcity
- abundance
- comfort
- health
- control
- hospitality
- tradition
- pleasure

The same food can carry very different meanings for different people—or different meanings for the same person at different times.

The important Week 1 question is not:

**Which meanings are correct?**

It is:

## How did food acquire meaning in my life?

# EXPERIENCE 1 — WHO TAUGHT YOU TO EAT?

Complete **Who Taught You to Eat?**

This experience traces the people, environments, institutions, events, and cultural messages that participated in shaping your relationship with food.

Its purpose is not to assign blame or determine which influences were right or wrong.

Its purpose is to make the history visible.

### Artifact

**Food Influence Map**

Keep the map available for the reflection that follows.

---

# REFLECTION 1 — LOOK BEFORE YOU EXPLAIN

Spend a moment looking at your Food Influence Map before trying to interpret it.

Consider:

1. What surprised you?
2. Which influences were so ordinary that you had never thought of them as influences before?
3. Which influences appear especially prominent?
4. What mattered strongly earlier in life but carries less weight now?
5. What still seems present today?
6. Are there influences you had previously experienced as simply “me” that now look as though they also have a history?

### Reflection

**What had you previously thought was simply part of you that you can now see was also learned, modeled, reinforced, experienced, or inherited?**

There is no requirement to decide what to do with that information yet.

# ACT IV — THE MIRROR WAS FURNISHED TOO

Return to Nora in the cereal aisle.

She is still holding the two boxes.

But there is another layer underneath the decision.

Part of what makes one cereal feel more responsible may not actually be about cereal.

It may be about her body.

What she believes she should weigh.

What she thinks eating certain foods might do to her body.

What she has learned a disciplined body looks like.

What she imagines other people notice.

What she has learned health is supposed to look like.

Body image often feels intensely personal:

**This is simply how I feel about my body.**

But people also learn how to perceive, compare, categorize, and evaluate bodies inside a social world.

A body changes through childhood.

Puberty changes it again.

Clothing sizes appear.

Peers notice bodies.

Adults comment.

Healthcare weighs and categorizes.

Sports may reward particular shapes, sizes, abilities, or performances.

Media presents bodies as ideals, warnings, transformations, before-and-after stories, and evidence of character.

Romantic and sexual relationships can introduce another gaze.

Pregnancy, illness, disability, aging, menopause, injury, medication, and weight changes may alter both the body and how it is experienced.

Social media adds a near-continuous stream of bodies against which one's own can be compared.

Again, the Week 1 question is not:

**How do I fix my body image?**

It is:

## Who—or what—helped teach me how to look at my body?

---

# EXPERIENCE 2 — HOW WAS MY BODY IMAGE CREATED?

Complete **How Was My Body Image Created?**

This experience traces the experiences and influences that participated in shaping how you learned to see, evaluate, and think about your body.

It is not asking you to love your body, replace negative thoughts with positive ones, or decide whether every body belief is accurate.

For now, the work is simply to make the origins visible.

### Artifact

**Body Image Influence Map**

Keep this map beside your Food Influence Map.

---

# REFLECTION 2 — TWO MAPS, ONE PERSON

Place the two maps conceptually beside one another:

**My Food Influence Map**

and

**My Body Image Influence Map**

Look for relationships without forcing conclusions.

Consider:

1. Where do the maps overlap?
2. Where are they surprisingly different?
3. Did some people, institutions, experiences, or cultural messages influence both?
4. Did food acquire meaning partly because of what you learned about bodies?
5. Did body image acquire meaning partly through experiences involving food, dieting, weight, exercise, or health?
6. Which influences still feel close?
7. Which feel more historical now?
8. What becomes easier to understand when you stop assuming your current relationship with food and body appeared from nowhere?

The point is not to create one perfect causal story.

Human frameworks are usually constructed through many overlapping experiences.

# ACT V — RETURN TO NORA

Nora is still in the grocery aisle.

The cereal boxes have not changed.

We have not told her which cereal to buy.

We have not determined whether either choice is healthier.

We have not decided that the voice telling her to “be good” is wrong.

She looks again at the red card.

## BE GOOD

And underneath it:

## WHO SAYS?

At the beginning of the week, **Who says?** could sound like a challenge.

Now it can become an actual question.

Who *did* say?

Maybe a parent.

Maybe a doctor.

Maybe a coach.

Maybe a health class.

Maybe Weight Watchers.

Maybe a magazine.

Maybe social media.

Maybe friends.

Maybe something Nora concluded after an experience of her own.

Probably more than one thing participated.

Nora does not have to decide today whether those influences were right or wrong.

For now, she has discovered something more basic:

## The voice in the grocery aisle has a history.

We leave Nora there.

The cereal choice remains unresolved.

# WEEK 1 INTEGRATION — YOUR FRAMEWORK BEGINS

This week creates the first layer of the **Personal Food & Body Framework Map**.

## Layer 1A — Food History

Carry forward the major influences and patterns that seem important from your **Food Influence Map**.

Do not try to carry every answer.

## Layer 1B — Body Image History

Carry forward the major influences and patterns that seem important from your **Body Image Influence Map**.

Again, this is not a permanent identity statement. It is a record of influences you can currently see.

Together, these establish:

**My food framework has a history.**

**My body-image framework has a history.**

That is enough for Week 1.

# MEANING — WHAT WEEK 1 HAS DONE

Your relationship with food has a history.

Your relationship with your body has a history.

Seeing that history does not tell you what to keep, reject, or change.

It does something that has to happen first:

## It makes the framework visible.

This is the first layer of the framework you will build throughout the course.

In the coming weeks, we will look at other parts of the system—not to find one master explanation, but to make more of what participates in food and body decisions available for examination.

There is another source of information participating in this system that existed before most of these rules and messages:

## Your body.

The next question is not whether you are good at listening to it.

It is more basic:

**What does your body actually tell you—and what happens after the signal arrives?**

# FACILITATOR / DESIGN NOTES

## Week 1 learning objectives

By the end of Week 1, participants should be able to:

1. Recognize that present food meanings developed through learning and experience.
2. Recognize that body-image frameworks also developed through learning and experience.
3. Identify multiple influences that participated in each framework.
4. Distinguish identifying an influence from deciding whether that influence was good, bad, true, false, helpful, or harmful.
5. Begin constructing the Food History and Body Image History layers of the Personal Food & Body Framework Map.

## Hidden critical-thinking function

Week 1 develops **framework/source awareness**.

The participant is not yet asked to critique the framework. The instructional move is earlier and more foundational:

**What I experience as obvious, natural, personal, or simply true may have a developmental and social history.**

Do not explicitly teach critical-thinking levels to participants.

## Boundary checks

Do not let Week 1 drift into:

- belief deconstruction — Week 3
- signal versus interpretation — Week 2
- multicausal analysis of current eating — Week 4
- scarcity mechanisms — Week 5
- behavioral function — Week 6
- environmental constraint analysis — Week 7
- framework revision — Week 8

## Story rule

Nora is not a case study to diagnose. She is the narrative thread that gives the participant a concrete human question before the conceptual material arrives.

Do not resolve the cereal decision in Week 1.

## Required interactive experiences

1. **Who Taught You to Eat?**
2. **How Was My Body Image Created?**

The curriculum should teach enough to make each discovery possible, but should not duplicate the discovery work performed by the tools.

## Week 1 artifact

**Personal Food & Body Framework Map — History Layer**

Composed of:

- Food History
- Body Image History

Only major influences/patterns carry forward. Avoid permanent labels or identity statements.
