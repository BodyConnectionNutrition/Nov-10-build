We live in environments that shape what we eat, how we feel, what we fear, and what we believe about our bodies. Then, when those environments contribute to distress, we are often told that the problem is inside us. We need more discipline, more resilience, better habits, better tracking, or another product to correct ourselves. The proposed solution is individual. The conditions in which the problem develops are left largely untouched.

That pattern raises a political question as much as a health question: **Who benefits when people are convinced they are broken?**

This is not an argument that illness is imaginary or that medication is inherently harmful. Obesity and mental health conditions are real; appropriate medical treatment can relieve suffering and increase a person's capacity to participate in life. It is an argument that biological treatment and structural explanation are not mutually exclusive. A treatment can help an individual while the economic arrangements contributing to widespread distress remain in place.

## When the environment disappears from the diagnosis

Consider the food environment. Food is aggressively marketed, convenience is engineered, and purchasing decisions are shaped by pricing, placement, availability, time pressure, and repeated exposure. People must make food decisions within those conditions, not outside them. Yet the cultural explanation for eating difficulties frequently lands on individual willpower. The body becomes the suspect, while the environment is treated as neutral.

Something similar can happen in mental health. Economic insecurity, exhausting work, social isolation, discrimination, unstable housing, inadequate sleep, and digitally mediated comparison can intensify distress. None of these conditions eliminates the reality of psychiatric illness. But a system focused almost exclusively on individual diagnosis and treatment can make the context invisible.

The World Health Organization calls attention to the **commercial determinants of health**: the ways business practices and commercial power influence health through products, marketing, working conditions, research, and policy. This is not simply a story about personal choices. It is also a story about who gets to design the choices available. See the [WHO's overview of commercial determinants of health](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/commercial-determinants-of-health).

When people interpret the consequences of these environments as personal inadequacy, they may devote more attention, money, and effort to correcting themselves. Commercial solutions proliferate: supplements, programs, tracking devices, diets, and medications. Some interventions are useful; others are unsupported or misleading. What they share is that they are sold in a marketplace in which distress itself can become demand.

This is one possible form of **structural subjugation**. It need not involve a single coordinated plan. Institutions can reinforce one another through incentives: create or intensify difficult conditions, individualize their consequences, sell remedies, and leave the conditions largely intact. Whether this cycle also reduces collective political action is an important empirical question, not something we can simply assume.

## A concrete example: the 1994 supplement law

The Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act of 1994, or **DSHEA**, shows how commercial interests can influence the rules of a health marketplace. Two leading Senate champions were Republican Orrin Hatch of Utah and Democrat Tom Harkin of Iowa. The industry's campaign also involved Representative Bill Richardson, who sponsored a House bill.

In September 1993, *The Washington Post* reported that supplement-industry leaders had raised **$80,000 for Hatch's reelection campaign at a fundraising brunch**. It reported that Hatch owned shares in a Utah supplement distributor, that three former aides had become industry lobbyists, and that important portions of proposed legislation closely resembled an industry lawyer's draft. Hatch disputed the suggestion that financial gain motivated his position and argued that he was defending consumers and an important Utah industry. [Read the contemporary investigation](https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1993/09/14/in-the-vitamin-wars-industry-marshals-an-army-of-citizen-protesters/4cb22cd1-4806-4331-b0d7-58f11b9e0b4a/).

In July 1994, the *Los Angeles Times* reported that the industry had spent **more than $2 million lobbying Congress** for the Hatch and Richardson bills, citing an analysis of public records. The campaign did not rely solely on private meetings. Trade groups enlisted health-food stores, distributed prewritten appeals, organized store demonstrations, and framed the legislation as a fight for consumer access to supplements. [Read the 1994 reporting](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1994-07-02-mn-10973-story.html).

The industry's mobilization was effective partly because it spoke to a genuine concern: people did not want government to arbitrarily prevent them from buying products they valued. But **access to a product and evidence that the product works are different questions**. Industry messaging often merged them. A demand for scientific substantiation could be presented as a threat to consumer freedom.

The political relationships continued after passage. In December 2000, *The Washington Post* reported that supplement companies had given more than **$80,000 to Hatch and $72,000 to Harkin in direct campaign contributions since 1994**. These later contributions are evidence of continuing political relationships, **not** proof that those sums purchased the original 1994 law. Both senators described themselves as sincere supporters of supplements and defended their positions. [Read the reporting on Hatch and Harkin](https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/2000/12/25/unlikely-allies-aid-industry/4000205f-415a-4ce6-9988-cd32d91e63e4/).

The distinction matters. Campaign contributions and lobbying relationships do not, by themselves, establish bribery or a quid pro quo. They do, however, make it essential to ask whose interests had access to lawmakers, who helped write the policy, and who benefited from the resulting regulatory design.

## What DSHEA changed

Under DSHEA, dietary supplements generally **do not require FDA approval for safety and effectiveness before marketing**. Manufacturers are responsible for ensuring that products meet applicable safety and labeling requirements; FDA can take action against adulterated or misbranded supplements after they enter the market. There are additional requirements for certain new dietary ingredients, and companies cannot lawfully make whatever disease-treatment claims they want.

The distinction from prescription drugs is substantial. For many supplements, a company can begin selling without first demonstrating to FDA that its product is effective. The agency itself explains this regulatory arrangement in its [consumer guidance](https://www.fda.gov/food/dietary-supplements/information-consumers-using-dietary-supplements) and [questions and answers](https://www.fda.gov/food/information-consumers-using-dietary-supplements/questions-and-answers-dietary-supplements).

This means consumers are routinely asked to evaluate products in a market where premarket demonstration of effectiveness is not generally required. That responsibility is especially difficult to discharge when claims are wrapped in scientific language, testimonials, endorsements, and carefully constructed impressions of credibility.

The law did not abolish oversight, and supplements are not all useless or dangerous. Certain products have established uses, including correcting nutrient deficiencies. The issue is the **allocation of responsibility and evidentiary burden**: how much must a seller establish before making money, and how much uncertainty must a buyer navigate?

## Is this kleptocracy?

*Kleptocracy* usually refers to public officials exploiting governing authority for private enrichment. **Regulatory capture** describes a related but different problem: the institutions responsible for setting or enforcing public rules become disproportionately responsive to the industries they oversee.

The documented DSHEA history supports serious scrutiny of industry influence, revolving-door relationships, and potential conflicts of interest. It does not establish that Hatch and Harkin committed bribery, or prove that the entire law was enacted for their personal enrichment. The evidence is powerful enough without making claims it cannot support.

And the question extends beyond the supplement industry. When powerful commercial actors can help shape the standards governing their own products, the public may be left paying twice: first for products and services, and again for the health and social consequences of inadequate protection. The size and distribution of those costs must be assessed case by case.

## From personal blame to accurately located responsibility

Michel Foucault's work on disciplinary power helps illuminate another part of this story. Power need not operate only through overt commands. People can learn to supervise and judge themselves according to norms they did not choose. In health culture, that can mean monitoring the body as an endless project of correction, interpreting difficulty as failure, and treating the purchase of improvement as a personal duty.

The problem is not that people take responsibility for their health. The problem is **mislocated responsibility**. A person can respond to their circumstances without being held responsible for having created all those circumstances. Agency means the capacity to act within real conditions, not the moral obligation to overcome every constraint alone.

That is why the questions we ask matter. Instead of asking only, “Why can't I control myself?” we can ask: What happened? What conditions shaped it? Which explanations have evidence? What support is missing? Who designed this environment? Who profits from the current arrangement? What is genuinely mine to change, and what requires institutional or collective action?

This is not an invitation to abandon medical care or individual action. It is an invitation to stop confusing those responses with a complete account of causation.

**If people spend their time, money, and attention trying to repair themselves, how much capacity remains to question the conditions in which they live?**

The point is not to replace one simplistic story—“it's all your fault”—with another—“it's all a conspiracy.” It is to recover the context that commercial narratives so often leave out. Bodies are not separate from environments. Health decisions are not made in neutral conditions. And responsibility should follow power as well as behavior.
