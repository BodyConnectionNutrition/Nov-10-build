People are often told to listen to their bodies after spending years being rewarded for ignoring them.

Dieting teaches a different authority system. Eat because the clock permits it. Stop because the calories are gone. Reject hunger because it arrived “too soon.” Finish the approved portion because nothing else will be allowed.

Over time, eating becomes a negotiation between biological information and external rules.

## Internal cues are not simple meters

Hunger and fullness are constructed from multiple inputs: gastric sensations, circulating nutrients and hormones, sensory experience, expectation, learning, stress, sleep, medication, attention, and context.

They are not infallible commands. They are information.

Restriction complicates that information in at least three ways:

1. **Signals can become more extreme.** Long delays and inadequate intake allow hunger to intensify until eating feels urgent.
2. **Attention is directed away from the body.** Numbers and rules become the primary criteria for starting and stopping.
3. **Signals lose credibility.** Hunger is labeled deceptive, emotional, or evidence of weakness.

The person becomes highly skilled at following instructions while feeling increasingly unable to understand what their own body is communicating.

## Fullness is not the same as satisfaction

A stomach can feel physically occupied while the eating experience remains incomplete.

High-volume diet foods may create distension without providing adequate energy. A meal can meet a calorie rule while excluding the fat, carbohydrate, flavor, texture, cultural meaning, or pleasure needed for satisfaction. The person leaves the meal “full” and continues thinking about food.

That is often interpreted as irrational appetite. It may be a mismatch between physical volume and the broader conditions that allow eating to feel complete.

## Reconnection requires observation, not performance

Body awareness is not restored by demanding perfect hunger-and-fullness eating. That simply turns internal cues into a new diet.

A more useful practice is descriptive:

- What sensations appeared before eating?
- How long had it been since the last adequate meal?
- What happened to energy, mood, focus, or irritability?
- At what point did urgency decrease?
- Did the food provide satisfaction as well as volume?
- What made signals easier or harder to notice?

There are no grades in this process. Eating without clear hunger does not represent failure. Eating past fullness does not erase learning. The purpose is to rebuild a relationship with information that restriction taught the person to distrust.

The body has not forgotten how to communicate. But its messages may have been overridden, amplified by deprivation, or translated through years of fear. Hearing it again requires conditions in which the answer is allowed to matter.
