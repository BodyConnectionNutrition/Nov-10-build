*What we are overlooking when we treat psychological distress and body weight with medication*

**We are medicalizing some of the distress associated with living in environments that systematically produce it, without adequately examining or changing those environments.**

We ask people to regulate appetite, attention, emotions, sleep, productivity, relationships, and health while they navigate systems that compete for their time, money, and attention. When they struggle, the explanation too often begins and ends inside the individual.

Medication can be effective, appropriate, and lifesaving. But effective treatment is not a complete explanation of a problem. These fifteen categories identify conditions that deserve examination alongside individual physiology and clinical care. They do not all have the same evidentiary strength, and none should be treated as a universal explanation.

## 1. Economic conditions and material insecurity

- Income insecurity and chronic uncertainty about basic needs
- Housing instability, eviction risk, overcrowding, and homelessness
- Food insecurity and cycles of deprivation and availability
- Medical debt and healthcare-related financial strain
- Low wages and multiple-job households
- Unpredictable work schedules and lack of paid leave
- Childcare costs and shortages
- Transportation insecurity
- Economic inequality and unequal access to resources
- Administrative burdens of benefits, insurance, and employment

**Why it matters:** Chronic stress, anxiety, depression, sleep disruption, and constraints on health-related behavior.

## 2. The modern food environment

- Ubiquitous food marketing and purchasing cues
- Highly palatable, energy-dense products
- Large portions and value-pricing incentives
- Unequal geographic access to nutritious foods
- Aggressive food marketing to children
- Personalized digital advertising and food-delivery notifications
- Convenience-driven food systems responding to time scarcity
- Irregular opportunities to eat at work or school
- Restrictive diet culture and contradictory food rules
- Weight-loss marketing and body dissatisfaction
- Nutrition misinformation and conflicting health claims
- Moralization of food choices
- Weight stigma and anticipated judgment
- Commercialization of the broad and loosely defined term “food noise”

**Why it matters:** Eating patterns, weight trends, food preoccupation, stigma, and distress around food decisions.

## 3. Work, productivity, and time scarcity

- Long working hours and excessive workloads
- Low job control and high performance demands
- Workplace surveillance and productivity monitoring
- Shift work and circadian disruption
- Pressure to remain available outside work hours
- Job insecurity and precarious employment
- Insufficient breaks for food, movement, and recovery
- Long commutes
- Emotional labor and customer-facing demands
- Caregiving layered onto paid employment
- Expectation that every hour should be productive
- Lack of meaningful recovery time

**Why it matters:** Occupational stress, burnout, depression, disrupted sleep, and metabolic health.

## 4. Digital environments and the attention economy

- Variable-reward designs encouraging repeated checking
- Infinite scrolling and autoplay
- Engagement-optimizing algorithms
- Notifications and repeated interruptions
- Social comparison and curated success
- Appearance-focused content and body-image comparison
- Alarming or contradictory health information
- Misinformation and conflicting expert claims
- Cyberbullying and online harassment
- Social approval metrics
- Blurred boundaries between work and personal life
- Fragmentation of attention
- Targeted advertising that commercializes insecurity

**Why it matters:** Sleep, body dissatisfaction, attentional demands, and anxiety; effects differ by user and context.

## 5. Sleep and circadian environments

- Night and rotating shifts
- Artificial light exposure at night
- Noise pollution
- Overcrowded or unsafe housing
- Extreme indoor temperatures
- Early school start times
- Late-night work demands
- Caregiving interruptions
- Digital stimulation near bedtime
- Long commutes and insufficient time in bed
- Neighborhood safety concerns
- Economic pressure to sacrifice sleep

**Why it matters:** Mood regulation, appetite, cognition, metabolic health, and cardiovascular risk.

## 6. Built environments and opportunities for movement

- Car-dependent neighborhoods
- Missing sidewalks and protected cycling infrastructure
- Unsafe traffic conditions
- Limited parks and green space
- Inaccessible recreation facilities
- Neighborhood violence
- Poor public transportation
- Sedentary workplace design
- Inadequate disability accommodations
- Limited time or money for recreation
- Extreme heat and other weather barriers
- Social conditions that discourage outdoor activity

**Why it matters:** Physical activity, cardiometabolic health, participation, and psychological well-being.

## 7. Social isolation and erosion of community

- Few opportunities for informal interaction
- Geographic separation from extended family
- Loss of accessible gathering spaces
- Unaffordable social activities
- Work schedules interfering with relationships
- Isolation among older adults
- Loneliness among adolescents and young adults
- Weak neighborhood ties
- Limited intergenerational contact
- Inadequate practical support for caregivers
- Stigma around mental illness and disability
- Social exclusion and discrimination
- Digital communication replacing some face-to-face interaction

**Why it matters:** Loneliness, depression, anxiety, and diminished social support.

## 8. Childhood, parenting, and educational environments

- Childhood poverty and material insecurity
- Adverse childhood experiences
- Abuse, neglect, and domestic violence exposure
- Bullying and peer exclusion
- Inadequate support for parents and caregivers
- Unequal access to quality early education
- Excessive academic performance pressure
- High-stakes testing
- Inadequate school counseling
- Insufficient recess and unstructured play
- Early schedules conflicting with adolescent sleep
- Inadequate neurodevelopmental accommodations
- Punitive rather than developmentally appropriate discipline
- Weight stigma and appearance-based bullying
- Social media pressures during identity development
- Family stress from housing and employment instability

**Why it matters:** Anxiety, depression, trauma-related symptoms, learning, and developmental outcomes. Environmental demands can exacerbate ADHD-related difficulties without proving they cause ADHD.

## 9. Trauma, violence, and threats to safety

- Domestic violence and coercive control
- Sexual violence and harassment
- Childhood abuse
- Community violence
- Workplace intimidation and harassment
- Discrimination and identity-based threats
- Unsafe housing and neighborhoods
- War, displacement, and forced migration
- Institutional abuse
- Repeated threatening or humiliating interactions
- Institutional failure to respond to reports of harm
- Economic dependence limiting escape from unsafe situations
- Inadequate trauma-informed healthcare
- Chronic uncertainty about safety

**Why it matters:** PTSD, depression, anxiety, sleep disturbance, pain, and emotional regulation.

## 10. Healthcare systems that manufacture or intensify health anxiety

- Disease mongering and expansion of perceived treatment needs
- Overdiagnosis of abnormalities unlikely to cause harm
- Low-value or excessive testing
- Direct-to-consumer tests with questionable clinical utility
- Continuous biomonitoring without clear benefit
- Risk calculators presented without adequate context
- Fear-based pharmaceutical advertising
- Wellness marketing that encourages suspicion of ordinary bodily processes
- Normal variation presented as dysfunction
- Commercial screening packages outside established recommendations
- Fragmented care and contradictory medical advice
- Weight-centered encounters that overlook other complaints
- Unexplained patient-portal alerts and abnormal results
- Financial conflicts of interest favoring products, tests, or procedures

**Why it matters:** Health anxiety, unnecessary intervention, cost, mistrust, and excessive symptom monitoring. Evidence is strongest for specific practices, not a claim that all healthcare manufactures anxiety.

## 11. Perfectionism, optimization, and cultural expectations

- Constant self-improvement as an obligation
- Productivity as a measure of worth
- Appearance equated with discipline or health
- Thinness and muscularity as social ideals
- Idealized parenting standards
- Pressure to optimize sleep, food, exercise, and cognition
- Self-tracking framed as responsibility
- Commercialized mindfulness and self-care
- Idealized careers, relationships, bodies, and lifestyles
- Comparison with curated examples of success
- Moral judgments attached to rest and convenience
- Expectation that every decision should be optimal
- Personal responsibility narratives detached from structural constraints

**Why it matters:** Perfectionism, shame, body dissatisfaction, and stress. Evidence differs across specific cultural mechanisms.

## 12. Gender expectations and unequal care responsibilities

- Unequal unpaid domestic labor
- Disproportionate caregiving obligations
- Workplace discrimination
- Sexual harassment
- Gendered appearance expectations
- Reproductive healthcare barriers
- Inadequate pregnancy and postpartum support
- Menstrual and menopausal symptoms dismissed or inadequately addressed
- Expectations of emotional caregiving
- Pressure on men to suppress vulnerability
- Gender-based violence
- Financial dependence and unequal resources

**Why it matters:** Distress, sleep loss, chronic stress, and barriers to treatment.

## 13. Environmental exposures and climate conditions

- Air pollution
- Traffic and industrial noise
- Lead and other neurotoxic exposures
- Extreme heat
- Inadequate indoor heating and cooling
- Wildfire smoke
- Flooding and natural disasters
- Environmental displacement
- Unsafe drinking water
- Substandard housing, dampness, and mold
- Limited access to green spaces
- Unequal exposure to industrial and occupational hazards
- Uncertainty and anxiety around environmental threats

**Why it matters:** Physical illness and some psychological or neurological outcomes; strength of causal evidence varies by exposure.

## 14. Political, institutional, and structural conditions

- Discriminatory policies and practices
- Unequal educational and employment opportunities
- Inadequate social safety nets
- Barriers to disability benefits and accommodations
- Unaffordable healthcare
- Loss of autonomy in institutions and workplaces
- Unstable essential services
- Incarceration and its family effects
- Immigration-related uncertainty and family separation
- Institutional practices that undermine trust
- Limited participation in decisions affecting one’s life
- Unequal occupational and environmental risks
- Policies that individualize problems requiring collective solutions

**Why it matters:** Chronic stress, trauma exposure, unequal health outcomes, and reduced access to support.

## 15. Choice overload, uncertainty, and cognitive demands

- Enormous consumer choice sets
- Conflicting expert recommendations
- Rapidly changing health guidance
- Endless comparison opportunities
- Pressure to research every decision
- Fear of making the wrong choice
- Difficulty separating credible information from advertising
- Continuous competition for attention
- Self-management demands across many life domains
- Complex healthcare and insurance navigation
- Institutional complexity shifted onto individuals
- Uncertainty about outcomes
- Narratives equating theoretical options with genuine freedom

**Why it matters:** Perceived stress, rumination, and cognitive demands. Choice overload is context-dependent, not universal.


## What happens when we individualize these problems?

Anxiety can become solely a problem of emotional regulation while ongoing threats go unexamined. Weight can become solely a problem of appetite control while food systems, work schedules, and deprivation remain unchanged. Exhaustion can be labeled inadequate self-care while recovery remains structurally inaccessible.

Three processes deserve scrutiny: environments may produce or intensify distress; institutions may explain that distress primarily as individual dysfunction; and commercial systems may offer products to manage it. These processes overlap, but they should not be assumed to operate identically in every case.

The existence of a treatment market does not prove that an illness was manufactured. Equally, a medication’s effectiveness does not establish that the conditions contributing to illness are acceptable or inevitable.

**What would healthcare look like if changing harmful conditions were treated as seriously as prescribing treatment to people living within them?** Before we conclude that people are failing, we need to examine the conditions in which they are being asked to succeed.

## Evidence and interpretation

Evidence is strongest for several established social determinants, trauma exposure, food insecurity, sleep disruption, and specific harmful environmental exposures. Claims about generalized choice overload, attention-economy effects, or commercialization of particular experiences require more careful, outcome-specific evaluation. This article is a framework for investigation, not a claim that medications are unnecessary or that all conditions are environmentally caused.
