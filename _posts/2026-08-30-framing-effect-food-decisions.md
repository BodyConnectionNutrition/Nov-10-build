Would you rather buy beef labeled “90 percent lean” or beef labeled “10 percent fat”?

They can be the same beef. But they do not feel like the same decision.

## Every decision arrives inside a frame

Framing changes which part of reality receives your attention. “Ninety percent fat-free” emphasizes what is absent. “Contains ten percent fat” emphasizes what is present. “Save 200 calories” sounds like a gain. “Avoid gaining weight” invokes a loss.

The underlying facts may be equivalent, but human decisions are not made from facts alone. They are made from facts as presented.

Attention is limited. A frame tells your brain what matters in the scene. That is useful: every decision requires a boundary. But whoever supplies the boundary gains influence over the conclusion.

This is why the question itself can direct the answer. “How do I control my cravings?” assumes cravings are a control problem. “What is my body responding to?” opens a different investigation.

## What the frame leaves outside

Food companies use nutrient frames: no added sugar, reduced fat, high protein, guilt-free. Diet marketing uses time frames and moral frames: a reset, a cheat day, falling off the wagon, getting back on track.

Before-and-after photographs are frames too. They remove sleep, stress, money, illness, relationship changes, camera angle, selection bias, and what happened after the “after.” You are shown the part of the story that sells the intervention.

Health headlines often frame relative risk without absolute risk. “Risk doubled” sounds enormous. A change from one person in ten thousand to two people in ten thousand is also a doubling. Both descriptions may be technically accurate; they do not create the same emotional picture.

Frames can turn food into a moral test. A label like “guilt-free” does not merely promise a feature. It presumes guilt belongs in the category. “Earn your dessert” frames eating as a debt. “Burn it off” frames movement as punishment.

When the frame is repeated often enough, people may stop asking whether its central assumption is true. They begin solving the problem exactly as the seller defined it.

## Reverse the frame

Try reversing the frame.

If the label emphasizes what is absent, ask what is present. If a claim gives relative risk, ask for absolute risk. If a diet calls a behavior “cheating,” describe the behavior without the moral word. If an ad shows an after, ask what is outside the photograph.

Then ask: Is this the question I want to answer, or the question someone needs me to answer so I will buy?

A frame is never the entire picture. Learning to notice its edges is one way to recover what was cropped out—including your own needs.

## A question to carry with you

Think of a food decision that felt obvious. How might the wording, image, or comparison have made one part of the decision visible and another part disappear?

## Evidence and intent

The research above establishes cognitive mechanisms. It does not prove that every package, interface, campaign, or company intentionally uses a formally named bias. Observable design can be described directly; claims about a company’s intent require separate evidence of intent.
