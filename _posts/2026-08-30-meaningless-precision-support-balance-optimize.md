> **What Does That Even Mean? · Article 3 of 8**  
> [← Article 2](/blog/clinically-studied-ingredients-not-clinically-studied-product/) · [Series index](/what-does-that-even-mean/) · [Article 4 →](/blog/doctor-formulated-medical-grade-manufacture-authority/)

Some wellness claims sound specific because they name a body system. “Supports metabolism.” “Balances hormones.” “Promotes detoxification.” “Optimizes gut health.” The words gesture toward physiology, which makes the claim feel measurable even when no measurement has been identified.

The trick is that the verb rarely commits to an observable result.

### “Support” can absorb almost any evidence

In ordinary language, support implies that something useful is being done. In supplement advertising, it often functions as a low-resolution verb connecting an ingredient to a body process: supports immune health, supports energy, supports digestion, supports normal blood sugar.

The U.S. regulatory framework allows dietary supplements to make certain structure/function claims without FDA preapproval. A company may describe how a nutrient or ingredient is intended to affect the normal structure or function of the body, provided it has substantiation, notifies FDA as required, and uses the statutory disclaimer. The product cannot lawfully claim to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent disease unless it meets the requirements that apply to drugs or authorized health claims.

That distinction helps explain why consumers see so much “support” language. The word can suggest proximity to a health outcome while remaining on the structure/function side of the line.

The legal status of a phrase, however, does not make it useful for an individual decision. “Supports metabolism” could refer to supplying a vitamin that participates in an ordinary metabolic pathway. It could refer to a small change in energy expenditure. It could imply easier weight loss without saying so. Unless the advertisement defines the process and outcome, the consumer cannot tell which meaning is intended.

### “Balance” hides both the starting point and the direction

“Balances hormones” is even more slippery. Hormones are not a single substance with a universal ideal setting. They fluctuate across the day, menstrual cycle, lifespan, pregnancy, illness, sleep, energy availability, medication use, and many other conditions. Different hormones have different regulatory systems. A desirable change in one person could be unnecessary or harmful in another.

To evaluate a hormone claim, we would need to know which hormone, measured how, in whom, from what baseline, moving in which direction, by how much, and with what clinical consequence. “Balance” supplies none of that. It converts a complex endocrine system into the image of a crooked picture being straightened on a wall.

The claim is persuasive partly because it gives nonspecific symptoms a single explanatory story. Fatigue, irritability, appetite changes, poor sleep, and difficulty concentrating are real experiences with many possible causes. “Hormone imbalance” can make those experiences feel finally explained while moving the consumer toward a product before appropriate assessment has occurred.

### “Detoxification” names a process but not the alleged problem

The body does transform and eliminate substances through the liver, kidneys, lungs, gastrointestinal tract, skin, and cellular enzyme systems. That does not mean every product claiming to promote detoxification has demonstrated increased removal of a harmful substance or improved health.

A meaningful detoxification claim would identify the substance, exposure, biological pathway, measurement, and outcome. Without those elements, the word borrows credibility from real physiology while leaving the product's contribution undefined.

### “Optimize” requires a target

To optimize something is to move it toward a defined best state. “Optimizes wellness” avoids naming both the target and the standard. Wellness might mean sleep quality, symptom burden, nutrient adequacy, physical function, mood, blood pressure, or dozens of other outcomes. A product cannot demonstrate optimization without identifying what was optimized and compared with what.

These verbs are effective precisely because the consumer completes them. A person worried about weight hears “metabolism” and imagines easier weight loss. A person exhausted by menopause symptoms hears “hormone balance” and imagines relief. A person anxious about processed food hears “detoxification” and imagines accumulated danger leaving the body.

The advertisement did not have to make each promise. It supplied a physiologic word and allowed the audience's fears and hopes to write the rest.

The better question is: **What exactly changed, how was it measured, and would that change matter to someone like me?**
