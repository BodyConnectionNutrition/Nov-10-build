**Being accustomed to hardship is not evidence that hardship has ceased to exist.**

Human beings adapt to difficult circumstances. We adjust our routines, expectations, interpretations, and behavior to keep living within the conditions available to us. That ability is essential. It can also conceal the extent of the demands we are carrying. Functioning is not the same thing as flourishing. Surviving an environment does not establish that the environment is adequate.

This distinction is important in nutrition counseling because what looks like an individual behavior may be an accommodation to conditions that have become so familiar that neither patient nor clinician initially notices them.

## When difficult conditions become ordinary

A patient who routinely works through lunch may arrive concerned about overeating in the evening. They may explain their hunger as a lack of discipline. But the day also included a long stretch without adequate food, limited breaks, and perhaps caregiving responsibilities after work. Their eating makes more sense once those conditions are restored to the picture.

Another patient may have spent decades dieting. Counting, second-guessing, and judging food choices have become ordinary parts of eating. The patient may believe this mental effort is simply what responsible eating requires. They may not recognize the burden because they have lived with it for so long.

A third patient may describe constant exhaustion as normal. Their schedule allows little sleep, their income leaves limited room for food choices, and healthcare is difficult to access. They may say they are doing okay. That report deserves respect, but it does not eliminate the material constraints.

These examples are possibilities, not diagnoses. Eating patterns, fatigue, and food-related thoughts have multiple causes. The task is to investigate with patients rather than impose a single explanation.

## Adaptation changes expectations, not necessarily conditions

Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum's work on capabilities helps clarify why subjective satisfaction alone cannot establish whether people have sufficient opportunities to live well. People can adjust expectations to what appears attainable. They can find meaning and dignity under constrained circumstances while still lacking resources they need.

The concept of *adaptive preferences* does not mean that people are mistaken about their own lives. It means that preferences and expectations form within environments. A patient who says, “I don't need breakfast,” may genuinely prefer that schedule, may have learned to suppress hunger, may lack time or food, or may be responding to medication or symptoms. The statement alone cannot tell us which.

A clinician who automatically affirms the pattern misses an opportunity to understand it. A clinician who automatically challenges it makes the opposite mistake. Good care explores the meaning of the pattern and the choices realistically available.

## The body also adapts

Adaptation is not limited to conscious belief. Sleep loss, food restriction, stress, and repeated experiences can influence appetite, attention, mood, and behavior. The body responds to the conditions it encounters.

The concept of *allostasis* describes physiological adjustment to changing demands. Research on *allostatic load* examines the cumulative burden associated with repeated or prolonged stress responses, although measurement remains debated. A response that is useful for getting through a difficult day may carry costs when the demand is repeated for months or years.

Someone who has experienced unpredictable access to food may become attentive to opportunities to eat. Someone accustomed to rushed meals may continue eating quickly when circumstances change. Neither observation, on its own, establishes why an individual behaves as they do. It does establish why a moral explanation such as “poor self-control” is inadequate.

## Food noise and the normalization of internal conflict

Food noise is often used to mean persistent thoughts about food. But that phrase can include hunger, planning, restriction, learned rules, anticipated judgment, and competing responsibilities. These experiences are not interchangeable.

For someone who has lived with dietary restriction for years, hunger may arrive alongside an automatic command to resist it. Enjoyment may trigger guilt. Every choice may involve calculating what food means for body size, health, or worth. The mental traffic can become so familiar that it feels like a natural feature of eating.

Some persistent food-related thoughts have biological, psychiatric, medication-related, or other causes. We should not assume they are all learned. But we should not assume that the experience itself proves a defective appetite or a broken body, either.

A useful question is: **What have you come to regard as normal around food that you have never had an opportunity to examine?**

## Endurance is not consent

There is a broader institutional consequence to mistaking adaptation for well-being. When someone continues working despite exhaustion, their continued attendance may be treated as evidence that the workload is sustainable. When a family manages to stretch inadequate food resources, their resourcefulness can conceal the inadequacy of the resources. When patients meet complex healthcare demands, their compliance can obscure how burdensome those demands are.

**The ability to endure a condition can be mistaken for evidence that the condition is tolerable.**

Resilience and justice address different questions. Resilience concerns the capacity to adapt to adversity. Justice concerns whether people should have to endure that adversity and how resources, protections, and responsibility are distributed.

We can recognize a patient's skill in coping without treating the hardship that required it as acceptable. We can acknowledge individual choices without pretending those choices occur in neutral environments.

## Putting context back into nutrition care

The Body Connection Nutrition framework offers five connected practices for examining these experiences.

**Notice.** What happened before any judgment was attached? A patient felt intensely hungry in the evening.

**Interpret.** What explanation appeared? “I have no willpower.” That is an interpretation, not a measurement.

**Contextualize.** What conditions matter? Meal timing, food access, sleep, work schedules, medications, symptoms, stress, caregiving, and learned food rules may all influence the experience.

**Locate Responsibility.** Which conditions can the patient influence, and which require resources, accommodation, or institutional change? A patient may be able to bring a portable meal but cannot independently establish a workplace break policy.

**Respond.** What would care look like within the capacity and resources available today? Perhaps a different meal schedule, medical evaluation, help obtaining food, an accommodation, or simply a less punitive explanation of what happened.

This is not a demand for patients to monitor every meal or interrogate every thought. It is an opportunity to interrupt an automatic verdict and recover information that the verdict excluded.

## Do not make awareness another burden

Adaptation itself is not a pathology. Familiar routines may be protective, helpful, chosen, or no longer useful. Especially when experiences involve trauma or prolonged insecurity, challenging an established pattern without understanding its function can undermine the very agency we hope to support.

Patients do not need to become experts in structural inequality before receiving adequate care. They do not have to identify every influence on their behavior. They deserve practical support, respect for their experience, and opportunities to develop other responses when those responses become possible.

Sometimes changing a thought helps. Sometimes the necessary change is a reliable meal break, more income, a safer environment, medical treatment, or assistance with food access. No amount of reframing can substitute for every missing resource.

## What we should stop confusing

When people report that they are managing despite inadequate food, financial insecurity, chronic exhaustion, or difficult healthcare demands, we should take both parts of that report seriously. Their capacity to manage is real. So are the constraints.

A person's ability to endure hardship tells us something about their capacity to adapt. It does not establish that the hardship is acceptable.

**We should not confuse what people have learned to live with, with what people need in order to live well.**
