Someone tells you that you should weigh 140 pounds. Or eat 1,200 calories. Or keep dinner under 500 calories. Where did that number come from?

It may not matter. Once you hear it, your brain has something to measure everything else against.

## When a starting point becomes a command

An anchor is an initial reference point. After it is introduced, later judgments tend to cluster around it—even when the original number was arbitrary or poorly suited to the person making the decision.

A suggested retail price makes the sale price feel cheap. A restaurant’s most expensive entrée makes the next one feel reasonable. A serving size makes a portion look correct. A calorie target makes eating above it feel excessive and eating below it feel successful.

The anchor does not have to be scientifically sound. It only has to arrive first.

Numbers need context. Your brain uses what is available to create that context. If you have no reason to know what a supplement should cost, the crossed-out price of $89 can make $49 feel like a bargain. If you do not know what your body will need today, a tracking app’s target can begin to feel more authoritative than hunger.

This is an efficient starting strategy. The danger comes when a starting point becomes a command.

## Numbers with borrowed authority

Diet culture is saturated with anchors: ideal weights, clothing sizes, “normal” BMIs, thirty-day timelines, daily step counts, macro percentages, and transformation photos labeled with pounds lost.

Food marketing uses price anchors and portion anchors. “Two for $6” encourages you to evaluate the deal instead of whether you wanted two. A giant restaurant portion can reset what a normal portion looks like. A tiny package labeled as one serving can do the reverse.

Even the language of progress is anchored. If a program promises ten pounds in a month, losing three may feel like failure—even if the original promise was unrealistic.

An anchor can turn bodily variation into disobedience. Hunger above a calorie target feels untrustworthy. A stable weight above a goal feels like evidence that the body is wrong. Money spent on a “discounted” supplement feels prudent because the inflated reference price did its job.

The harm is not only a bad calculation. It is the replacement of context with a number that was never entitled to control the decision.

## Remove the number and look again

Ask two questions:

Who chose the starting number?

What changes if I remove it?

If the “original price” disappeared, would the product still be worth the current price? If the calorie target vanished, what information would hunger, energy, medical needs, satisfaction, and the shape of your actual day provide?

Numbers can be useful tools. They become dangerous when their precision hides the weakness of their origin.

An anchor feels like reality because everything is arranged around it. Sometimes agency begins by asking whether the anchor should have been dropped there at all.

## A question to carry with you

What number has become a standard you judge your body or eating against? Who gave it authority, and what evidence says it belongs to you?

## Evidence and intent

The research above establishes cognitive mechanisms. It does not prove that every package, interface, campaign, or company intentionally uses a formally named bias. Observable design can be described directly; claims about a company’s intent require separate evidence of intent.
