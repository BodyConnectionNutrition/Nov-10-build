*The long journey from pomace to promise*

After grapes are crushed for wine or juice, a dense mass remains: skins, seeds, stems and pulp collectively called pomace. It is heavy, wet, biologically active and produced at enormous scale. It is also rich in fiber and contains polyphenolic compounds that survive pressing. That combination makes grape pomace both a disposal challenge and an attractive raw material [10].

## What the finished capsule promises

THE NAMED PRODUCT. NOW Foods describes its 100-mg grape-seed extract as at least 90% polyphenols and says its OPCs may support vascular, bone, kidney, brain and other tissues by supporting responses to oxidative and metabolic stress [26]. Each phrase is carefully framed as structure/function support, yet the combined impression is strikingly broad.

## From discarded seed to standardized capsule

Processors can dry and mill pomace into flour, separate oil from seeds, or extract and concentrate compounds such as proanthocyanidins. Grape-seed extract is now sold in capsules and incorporated into supplemented foods. Health Canada has formally evaluated standardized grape-seed extract as a supplemental ingredient under specified conditions, confirming that this is an established commercial use rather than merely a laboratory proposal [11].

## The chemistry that is genuinely measurable

NOW Foods accurately discloses a 100-mg grape-seed extract standardized to at least 90% polyphenols, including OPCs [26]. Grape seeds do contain proanthocyanidins, and a standardized extract can be chemically characterized [10,11]. Those are verifiable composition claims. They do not, by themselves, verify protection of the organs named in the marketing.

## What the human evidence can support

THE INDEPENDENT SUMMARY. NCCIH notes that grape-seed extract is promoted for blood pressure, cholesterol and other purposes, while summarizing a mixed and incomplete evidence base [27]. Even when meta-analyses detect a modest change in a risk marker, that does not establish protection of the brain, kidneys, bones and vascular system as a unified clinical outcome.

## How one compound acquires a multi-organ promise

This is where the economics of valorization and the language of wellness become mutually useful. Winemakers and juice processors gain a market for material that otherwise requires management. Ingredient companies gain a concentrated extract with measurable chemical properties. Supplement brands gain a plant-derived story that sounds both natural and scientific. Consumers receive the final narrative—but rarely the uncertainty between the assay and the health promise.

## The missing clinical bridge

The unresolved human-outcome question is whether this exact extract and dose meaningfully improve vascular, bone, kidney or brain health. Evidence concerning a biomarker such as blood pressure cannot be generalized automatically to protection across several organ systems, and NCCIH characterizes the evidence for promoted uses as incomplete [27].

## Why a broad health story is so valuable

Winemaking and juice production generate seeds and pomace that can be sold or processed instead of managed as low-value residue [10]. No reliable raw-seed price, extraction cost or product-margin data were obtained for this case. The economic conclusion is therefore limited: standardization converts a production remainder into a differentiated supplement ingredient, and a multi-organ support story can broaden its market.
