*The claim-making machine behind two different extracts*

Articles 4 and 5 examined two products. This article examines the claim-making machine they share. Grape-seed and citrus extracts begin with different raw materials, but both can travel from measurable chemistry to a health impression without evidence ever completing that journey.

## The claim-making machine they share

THE SHARED MOVE. Article 4 documented grape-seed language about free-radical scavenging and support across several tissues [26]. Article 5 documented citrus language about neutralizing free radicals and maintaining cellular health [28]. The products differ; the inference is the same. A true statement about composition or chemical activity is positioned close enough to body language that the consumer completes the missing clinical step.

## Four statements that are not interchangeable

Four statements must remain separate: (1) the ingredient contains antioxidant compounds; (2) the ingredient reacts in a laboratory assay; (3) consuming it changes a validated human biomarker; and (4) consuming it improves a meaningful health outcome. Each statement requires different evidence. Proving the first or second does not prove the third or fourth.

## What the assay legitimately establishes

The first two statements can be entirely legitimate. The named ingredients contain polyphenolic compounds, and properly performed assays can characterize their behavior under specified chemical conditions [5,26,28]. Those findings support the verbs ‘contains’ and ‘reacts.’ They do not support ‘protects,’ ‘prevents’ or ‘improves’ without additional human evidence.

## Where evidence must cross into the body

THE EVIDENCE LADDER. A defensible sequence is chemical identity; activity in an assay; release during digestion; absorption and metabolites; relevant tissue exposure; change in a validated human biomarker; and, finally, a meaningful clinical outcome. Evidence can stop at any rung. It cannot be promoted upward by changing the wording on a label [5,6].

## How one rung is promoted as the next

The marketing shortcut is to place evidence from one rung beside language from the next. ‘Contains polyphenols’ lends authority to ‘antioxidant activity’; antioxidant activity lends authority to ‘cellular support’; and cellular support encourages the consumer to imagine protection from illness. The sentences may remain technically distinct while their arrangement produces a clinical conclusion the studies never tested.

## The human endpoint that remains missing

The missing statement is the fourth one: consuming this product improves a meaningful human health outcome. Evidence of composition, assay activity or even a biomarker change cannot establish reduced disease, organ protection or clinically meaningful immune benefit. Those conclusions require product-specific human trials with relevant doses, comparators and endpoints [5,6].

## Why numbers make weak claims look strong

Numbers such as polyphenol percentage, assay values and standardized concentrations give ingredient suppliers precise points of differentiation. This investigation did not establish that an antioxidant score produces a particular price premium. The narrower commercial inference is that numerical chemistry makes similar extracts easier to distinguish and makes a health impression appear more thoroughly measured than it is.
