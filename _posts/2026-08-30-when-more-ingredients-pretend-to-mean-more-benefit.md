A number is one of the easiest ways to make a supplement look substantial. Seventy-five ingredients. Eighty nutrients. Dozens of vitamins, minerals, botanicals, probiotics, adaptogens, and “superfoods” in a single scoop. The number lands before the consumer has time to ask what any of those ingredients are doing there.

AG1, for example, currently promotes a daily drink containing more than 75 ingredients to support energy, gut health, and immune defense. The company also provides considerably more information than the ingredient count alone: a Supplement Facts panel, explanations of ingredient categories, safety testing, and descriptions of research. That matters. But the number remains one of the product's most visible persuasive features because abundance is easy to interpret as value.

The leap feels intuitive: if three useful ingredients are good, 75 must be extraordinary. Yet a formulation is not a ballot in which every ingredient casts one vote for health. The presence of an ingredient tells us almost nothing without its chemical form, amount, stability, bioavailability, intended population, and relationship to the other ingredients in the product.

### An ingredient list is not a list of benefits

A label can establish that a substance is present. It cannot, by itself, establish that the finished product produces a meaningful outcome.

Some ingredients may be present primarily to supply familiar nutrients. Some may contribute flavor, texture, preservation, or color. Some may have evidence at a dose that is difficult to fit into a multi-ingredient scoop. Some may have been studied individually but never in the final combination. Some may be useful only for people with a specific deficiency or inadequate intake. Others may be included in amounts that are technically present but unlikely to reproduce the research consumers associate with the ingredient's name.

This problem becomes especially difficult when a product uses a proprietary blend. Under U.S. labeling rules, the total weight of the blend and the ingredients in descending order by weight may be disclosed without stating the amount of every individual ingredient. Researchers from the National Institutes of Health and other institutions have noted that this makes it impossible to calculate exposure or determine whether a labeled product supplies the dose used in a clinical trial.

Even when every amount is disclosed, the consumer still has work to do. More is not automatically better. Nutrients have different dose-response relationships, upper limits, interactions, and consequences for people taking medications or managing health conditions. A long label can therefore create the appearance of completeness while making the product harder to evaluate.

### Complexity can substitute for comparison

Ingredient-count marketing also distracts from the comparison that actually matters. Compared with what?

If the product is intended to fill nutrient gaps, the relevant question is whether the person has those gaps and whether the formula addresses them in appropriate amounts. If it is intended to improve energy, digestion, sleep, mood, or immunity, the relevant question is whether the finished product improved a defined outcome more than a credible comparison under controlled conditions.

Without that comparison, the number of ingredients is mostly product architecture. It tells us how complicated the formula is, not how much benefit it produces.

This distinction is easy to lose because the language of abundance feels concrete. “Seventy-five ingredients” is measurable. “Whole-body health” feels comprehensive. Together they produce a conclusion without stating one directly: this product must do more because it contains more.

But a formulation can be sophisticated and effective with a small number of well-chosen ingredients. It can also be crowded, underdosed, redundant, or irrelevant with a very large number. The count cannot distinguish between those possibilities.

### The question the advertisement avoided

When a company leads with the size of the ingredient list, do not begin by counting. Ask what each category is supposed to accomplish, whether the amounts are disclosed, and whether the finished formulation has been tested for the outcome being implied.

The useful question is not, “How many ingredients does it contain?”

It is: **What evidence shows that this exact combination, in these amounts, provides more benefit than a simpler or less expensive alternative?**
