Food can become loud long before anyone takes a bite.

It appears in the background of a meeting. It interrupts a conversation. It follows someone through the grocery store and into bed. The person may call this food noise, obsession, or lack of control.

But a deprived brain is supposed to prioritize food.

## The body does not passively accept an energy deficit

When energy intake falls and weight is lost, the body responds with coordinated adaptations designed to close the gap. Hunger rises. Satiety signaling changes. Energy expenditure declines. Food becomes more motivationally important.

In a controlled weight-loss study, participants lost about 10% of their body weight. One year later, several appetite-related hormonal changes remained significantly different from baseline, and participants continued to report greater hunger. The response included lower leptin and changes in peptide YY, cholecystokinin, insulin, ghrelin, and other signals.

This is not the body sabotaging a morally worthy plan. It is regulation.

## Hunger changes what captures attention

Hunger is not simply an empty sensation in the stomach. It changes information processing.

In neuroimaging research, caloric deprivation increased responsiveness to food cues in brain regions involved in attention, motivation, and reward. Other experiments find that people report stronger cravings when hungry than when satiated.

The food environment has not necessarily become more persuasive. The biological state of the person encountering it has changed.

This matters because people are often told to prove control in the state least compatible with effortless control. They are asked to spend the day eating as little as possible and then blamed when food commands their attention at night.

## Food noise is information

Food noise can have multiple contributors: environmental cues, learned reward, stress, habit, medication effects, sleep loss, food insecurity, and individual neurobiology. It should not automatically be reduced to energy deprivation.

But it also should not automatically be interpreted as addiction, greed, or a defective relationship with food.

Questions worth asking include:

- Has enough food been eaten today?
- Has eating been delayed for long stretches?
- Has recent weight loss increased biological pressure to eat?
- Are meals physically filling but missing adequate energy or satisfaction?
- Is the person surrounded by foods they are required to resist but never allowed to enjoy?

Sometimes the mind keeps returning to food because the body is still waiting to be fed.

## The contradiction at the center of dieting

Restriction strengthens the drive while demanding greater resistance to it.

The person then experiences the predictable result—more thoughts, stronger urges, greater cue reactivity—as proof that they cannot be trusted. The diet becomes both the producer of the problem and the proposed solution.

Your body did not betray you by making food difficult to ignore. It responded to inadequate energy by elevating the importance of obtaining energy.
