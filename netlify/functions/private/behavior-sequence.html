A guided learning experience

Behavior Sequence

Reconstruct one food-related behavior from the conditions that existed beforehand through what happened afterward.

This tool is not deciding whether the behavior was good or bad. It is restoring the parts of the story that disappear when we look only at the action itself.

Behavior is often the end of a sequence—not the beginning of an explanation.

1 · Choose one episode

Start with one specific moment.

Choose something recent enough that you can remember some of what surrounded it. It does not need to be dramatic.

What behavior are you looking at?
Before looking at the sequence, how would you have explained why it happened?

2 · Restore the sequence

The behavior is one part of the story.

When we begin with the action—I ate it, I skipped it, I kept going, I lost control—everything that came before can disappear. A sequence asks a different question: what was already happening by the time the behavior occurred?

Not every episode contains every step. Leave something blank if you do not remember it or it does not seem relevant. Do not invent a link just to complete the map.

3 · Conditions

What was already true before the moment began?

Look farther back than the immediate trigger. Conditions can accumulate quietly.

Anything else that was already true?

4 · Cue or event

What happened just before things shifted?

A cue may be external, internal, social, or simply a transition. Sometimes there is no obvious single cue.

What do you remember happening?

5 · Body state

What was happening in your body?

Notice what you remember without requiring yourself to know exactly what every sensation meant.

Anything else you remember physically?

6 · Thought or interpretation

What meaning or instruction appeared?

This is not a belief audit. We are simply restoring what your mind contributed in this particular sequence.

What words, images, expectations, or interpretations do you remember?

7 · Urge or pull

Was there a sense of momentum?

An urge can be strong, faint, conflicted, or absent. The point is not to rate your control; it is to notice whether a pull toward or away from action was part of the sequence.

Anything you want to add about the urge?

8 · Action

Now place the behavior back into the sequence.

Describe what happened without turning the description into a verdict about you.

What did you do?
Description is enough. “I ate three cookies while standing in the kitchen” gives the map more information than “I was bad.”

9 · Immediate result

What changed right away?

Consequences can help explain why a behavior becomes easier to repeat. They do not tell us whether the behavior was good or bad.

What was different immediately afterward?

10 · Later result

What showed up later?

Immediate and later consequences can be different. Both can belong in the same explanation.

Anything else that happened later?
