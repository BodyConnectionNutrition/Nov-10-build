A model before we measure

A food decision is more than the final action.

Your body can produce hunger while a rule says it is too early to eat. Food can become highly salient while you are actively trying not to eat it. A meeting can prevent a response to hunger even when no restriction is present. Repeatedly overriding a signal can itself become a practiced sequence.

These forces can operate together. The later eating episode may have a causal history that began hours before the first bite.

The central question is: what happened between my body's signals and what I ultimately did?

Driver 1 · Biology

What was your body signaling?

How physically hungry were you?
How long since meaningful nourishment?
Were you tired, stressed, active, sleep-deprived or depleted?
What happened when you noticed hunger?

Not eating is also behavior.

Hunger is information, not a command, but what happens after the signal matters. A signal can be answered, delayed by circumstances, overridden by a rule, distrusted, or missed entirely. Those are different pathways and should not be collapsed into one explanation.

Driver 2 · Reward & food noise

How did food capture attention?

Did seeing, smelling, encountering or thinking about food intensify wanting?
How appealing did food seem?
If you ate, how strong was the pull toward the next bite?
If you did not eat, what happened to your attention toward food?

Food noise does not require eating. A person can experience intense food attention while actively preventing themselves from eating. Biological need, learned reward and psychological scarcity can all increase salience.

Driver 3 · Restriction & learned rules

What rules shaped whether you responded?

A rule can shape eating by encouraging it or preventing it. Cognitive restriction matters even when a person ultimately does not eat. The gap between “my body signaled hunger” and “I decided I was allowed to respond” is itself useful information.

Driver 4 · Habit & automaticity

Was this response practiced?

In similar situations, how often do you respond in the same way—eat, delay, skip, or keep going?
Did the response happen with little deliberation?

The sequence is context → cue or body signal → action → consequence. The action might be eating. It might also be postponing lunch, drinking coffee instead, continuing a meeting, or automatically deciding hunger can wait.

What action did you take?
What changed afterward?

Driver 5 · Emotion & regulation

What function did the action serve?

Eating can regulate emotion, but so can not eating. Control, safety, virtue, accomplishment, avoidance and relief can become consequences that reinforce delaying or overriding body signals.

Driver 6 · Environment & access

What did the world around you make possible?

How easy was it to access food that could meet your needs?
How strongly did the environment cue or make eating easy?

“I chose not to eat” and “I could not reasonably eat” are not the same pathway. Access, money, work, caregiving and time can interrupt the connection between a body signal and a response without cognitive restriction being the cause.

Driver 7 · Relationships & culture

How did other people shape the response?

How strongly did other people or social expectations influence whether, what or when you ate?

Relationships can encourage eating and suppress it. We learn not only what food means, but when eating is acceptable, who is allowed to express hunger, and whether responding to the body feels socially safe.
