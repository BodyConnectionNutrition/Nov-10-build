Who Taught You to Eat?

Your relationship with food did not appear fully formed. It accumulated through feeding, family life, culture, peers, authority, media, adulthood, and repeated experience. This tool helps you see some of what participated in teaching you what eating means.

You are not looking for one cause, and you are not assigning blame. In each section, select experiences that actually fit your life. Then rate how much that area still influences your day-to-day eating now. Something can have happened without carrying much influence today—and something seemingly small can still carry a great deal.

Early childhood

1 · Your early food world

This section is about how eating actually worked when you were young: who decided when food happened, how hunger and fullness were handled, what access looked like, what happened around unfamiliar foods, and what you learned from watching the adults around you.

Select experiences you personally remember having, seeing, or feeling. You do not need to select anything that does not fit.

How much does your early food world influence your eating today?

Childhood through adulthood

2 · Family, culture, and belonging

This is different from the first section. Here we are exploring what food meant: love, hospitality, tradition, religion, identity, family roles, celebration, respect, belonging, and the social meaning attached to eating.

Select meanings and traditions that were genuinely part of your food world.

How much do family, cultural, and belonging-related meanings influence your eating today?

School years and adolescence

3 · Growing up around other eaters

As your social world expanded, eating became more public. School, friends, sports, puberty, health education, comparison, early dieting culture, and eating in front of other people could introduce new ideas about what was normal, admirable, embarrassing, healthy, excessive, or acceptable.

Select experiences from school years or adolescence that affected how you learned to eat.

How much do peer, school, and adolescent experiences influence your eating today?

Adolescence through adulthood

4 · Authority and expertise

Some food lessons arrive with authority behind them. Healthcare, nutrition education, dieting systems, fitness culture, tracking tools, and wellness programs can change what feels medically necessary, scientifically correct, disciplined, dangerous, or responsible.

Select experiences that materially shaped how you learned to evaluate food or eating. This is about what you were taught—not whether the advice was right or wrong.

How much do formal health, nutrition, fitness, or wellness systems influence your eating today?

Across the lifespan

5 · Media and commercial food culture

Food learning also happens through repetition. Advertising, social media, television, packaging, restaurants, apps, before-and-after stories, wellness trends, and commercial promises can teach what looks normal, desirable, excessive, virtuous, convenient, sophisticated, or shameful—even when no one explicitly tells us what to believe.

Select repeated messages or experiences that became part of your food environment.

How much do media and commercial messages influence your eating today?

Adulthood and current life

6 · Adult experiences that kept teaching you

Food learning does not stop in childhood. Independence, relationships, parenting, illness, work, financial changes, migration, caregiving, and major life transitions can create new routines and new meanings. Here we are focused on what these experiences taught you about eating—not on auditing every current logistical barrier.

Select adult experiences that changed what eating came to mean or how you learned to feed yourself.

How much do adult experiences and later learning influence your eating today?
